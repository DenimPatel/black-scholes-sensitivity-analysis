Every input you've been moving has a named sensitivity attached to it, and traders call these the Greeks.

Delta measures how much the option price moves per dollar move in the stock, and it doubles as a hedge ratio: short delta shares of stock against a long call to become delta-neutral. Gamma measures how fast delta itself changes, and it peaks for at-the-money options close to expiry, exactly where hedging gets hardest. Vega measures sensitivity to volatility, and it's largest for at-the-money, long-dated options. Theta is the time decay you just met. And rho measures sensitivity to the risk-free rate, usually the smallest effect of the five for short-dated options.

Explore the grid of charts: each tile sweeps one input and traces out the corresponding Greek. Notice where each curve peaks, and think about why: gamma and vega both live at the money, where the outcome is least certain and small changes in inputs swing the odds the most.

These five numbers are the actual working vocabulary of an options desk. Everything so far has come from a closed-form formula. Next, we'll price the same option a completely different way, by brute-force simulation, and watch it converge to the same answer.
